[SCENE 4 — TEACH · Point 1: Tokens] (0:55–2:40)
SLIDE (max 12 words): Tokens: The Atoms of Language Model Input and Output
DIAGRAM DESCRIPTION: Animated tokenization flow. A sentence appears at
the top: "The customer's refund of $149.99 was processed on 2024-03-15."
Below it, the sentence splits into colored token blocks:
["The", " customer", "'s", " refund", " of", " $", "149", ".99",
" was", " processed", " on", " ", "202", "4", "-03", "-15", "."]
A counter shows: "17 tokens." Below that, a cost calculator:
"GPT-4o input: $2.50 per 1M tokens → this sentence costs $0.0000425."
SCRIPT:
LLMs don't read words. They read tokens. A token is roughly three
quarters of a word. Sometimes a word is one token. Sometimes it's
three.

Watch this sentence: "The customer's refund of one hundred forty-nine
ninety-nine was processed on 2024-03-15." That looks like thirteen
words. But the model sees seventeen tokens. The dollar amount splits
into separate pieces. The date breaks apart. The apostrophe in
"customer's" creates an extra token.

Why does this matter for testing? Two reasons.

Cost. Every token costs money. GPT-4o charges two dollars fifty per
million input tokens. Run a test suite with five hundred test cases,
each with a two-thousand-token prompt? That's a million tokens — two
dollars fifty per run. Run it ten times during development? Twenty-five
dollars. Tokens are your testing budget.

Second — output length. When you set max tokens to fifty, you're not
limiting words. You're limiting tokens. Your agent might cut off
mid-sentence if the token boundary falls in the middle of a word. Your
tests need to account for truncation.

Here's the rule of thumb: one hundred tokens is roughly seventy-five
words. Memorize that ratio. You'll use it constantly.

[SCENE 5 — TEACH · Point 2: Context Windows] (2:40–4:30)
SLIDE (max 12 words): Context Windows: Everything the Model Sees at Once
DIAGRAM DESCRIPTION: A horizontal bar representing a 128K token context
window for GPT-4o. Segmented and color-coded from left to right:
  Segment 1 (orange): "System Prompt — 500 tokens"
  Segment 2 (blue): "Conversation History — variable"
  Segment 3 (green): "Retrieved Documents (RAG) — variable"
  Segment 4 (purple): "Tool Definitions — 800 tokens"
  Segment 5 (yellow): "User Query — 200 tokens"
  Segment 6 (gray, rightmost): "Response Space — what's left"
A red danger zone arrow shows what happens when segments overflow the window.
SCRIPT:
The context window is everything the model can see at once. Think of it
as the model's desk. Everything has to fit on the desk — the system
prompt, the conversation history, any retrieved documents, tool
definitions, the user's question, and the space for the response.

GPT-4o has a context window of one hundred twenty-eight thousand tokens.
Sounds massive. But watch how fast it fills up.

Your system prompt takes five hundred tokens. You define eight tools —
that's another eight hundred tokens just for the function schemas. The
user has a conversation history of twenty messages — five thousand
tokens. Your RAG pipeline retrieves four documents — six thousand
tokens. The user asks a new question — two hundred tokens.

You've used twelve thousand five hundred tokens. Still plenty of room.
But here's where it gets dangerous.

What if the conversation runs to a hundred messages? Now your history is
twenty-five thousand tokens. What if your RAG pipeline returns ten
documents instead of four? Another fifteen thousand tokens. Suddenly
you're at forty-one thousand tokens and the model starts losing track
of information in the middle of the window.

This is called "lost in the middle" — models pay the most attention to
the beginning and end of the context, and tend to ignore what's in the
center. For testing, this means your agent might give perfect answers
with short contexts and completely wrong answers with long ones. Your
test suite needs both.

[SCENE 6 — TEACH · Point 3: Temperature] (4:30–5:50)
SLIDE (max 12 words): Temperature: The Creativity Dial That Breaks Your Tests
DIAGRAM DESCRIPTION: A horizontal slider labeled "Temperature" ranging
from 0.0 to 2.0. At 0.0: label "Deterministic — same answer every time"
with an icon of identical outputs stacked. At 1.0: label "Creative —
varied, sometimes surprising" with diverse outputs fanning out. At 2.0:
label "Chaotic — often incoherent" with scattered, broken outputs. A
"sweet spot" marker at 0.3 labeled "Most production agents use this."
SCRIPT:
Temperature is a creativity dial. At zero, the model picks the most
likely next token every single time. Same input, same output.
Deterministic. Testable.

At one, the model samples from a wider distribution. It considers
less-likely tokens. The outputs get varied, sometimes surprising,
occasionally wrong. At two — which nobody uses in production — the
outputs become nearly random and often incoherent.

Here's the analogy. Temperature zero is like asking ten people to fill
in "the capital of France is ___" — everyone writes Paris. Temperature
one is like asking "name a city in France" — you'll get Paris, Lyon,
Marseille, Nice. All correct. All different. Temperature two is like
asking "name a place" — you might get Narnia.

Most production agents use a temperature between zero and zero point
three. Low enough for consistency, high enough to avoid sounding
robotic. But even at zero point three, you'll see variation across runs.
And that variation is exactly why exact-match assertions fail.

Temperature zero. Run one — "Items may be returned within thirty days of

three — identical again. At temperature zero, the model is a broken
record. Predictable. Easy to test.

Now temperature one. Run one — "You can return any item within thirty
days for a full refund." Different wording. Same meaning. Run two —
"Our policy allows returns up to thirty days after purchase with a
complete refund." Different again. Still correct. Run three — "Return
your item within thirty days of buying it to get your money back." Three
runs, three different sentences, all factually accurate.

An assertEqual test would fail on two out of three of those responses.
The agent is working perfectly. The test is broken. This is why agent
evaluation requires semantic comparison — checking meaning, not exact
words. And that's exactly what you'll build in Module 3.
